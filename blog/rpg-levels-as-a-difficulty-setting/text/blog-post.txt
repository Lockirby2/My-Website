Generally speaking, grinding levels in an RPG makes the game easier.  I know, big shock.  But at the same time, I feel that a lot of people don't exactly think of it this way.  Oftentimes, the mindset players have instead is either "This game is hard, so I need to grind some levels to win" or "This game is easy, so I can afford to skip some random encounters and still come out on top".  In other words, they think of difficulty as being intrinsic to the game rather than the way they're playing through the game.  Oddly, both mindsets are partially accurate.  For now, let's treat the difficulty of an RPG as being inversely correlated with how much players level.

Allowing players to grind in levels in an RPG has several big advantages over a traditional difficulty system.  The first is that the difficulty is granular, allowing players to tailor their experience to exactly what they want.  A traditional difficulty system often only has 3–5 choices, and it's hard to tell what your choice means at the outset.  In one game, "Easy" mode might be boring for you, while in another, "Hard" might be unpleasantly difficult.  On top of that, perhaps the experience you want is somewhere between "Easy" and "Normal", meaning that none of the options fit your tastes perfectly.  On top of that, maybe there's just one particular boss that you can't quite figure out.  In that case, you may want to fight that boss on "Easy" while playing the rest of the game on "Normal".  Allowing players to change the difficulty setting throughout the game alleviates those concerns somewhat, but players who are bored on "Easy" may not realize that their choice of difficulty is the reason why they're bored with the game and just drop it entirely.  Meanwhile, players who have played the game entirely on "Hard" may feel that they're wimping out by setting the difficulty to "Normal" for one boss.

This is where the true brilliance of levels shines: because leveling is a trade-off between time and power, leveling feels like an <i>in-game</i> choice rather than an <i>out-of-game</i> choice like a traditional difficulty selection.  If you're steamrolling everything because the strategies you're employing are beyond what you need to complete the game, you may feel that your good play has earned you the right to skip fighting some encounters.  If you see easy random encounters to mainly be a source of resources, skipping them feels more like a cost-benefit analysis and less like skipping a critical part of the core gameplay loop.  If the boss you're facing seems too daunting, you may decide that the reason why you can't beat it is because your characters are too weak and not because your strategy is lacking.  But because you're trading your time for the extra power, it feels like you've <i>earned</i> that victory even though you haven't had to gain skill to achieve it.

If players don't think about any of this, that's probably a good thing.  Most people would enjoy the game more if they feel that they've earned either their timesave or their tough victory without feeling as though they've skipped over some aspect of the game.  Of course, depending on your mindset about what it means to overcome a challenge, it's possible that the previous paragraph would shatter that illusion for you (sorry).  But I think people <i>developing</i> games would benefit from thinking of leveling in this way.

But this raises a question: if leveling is a difficulty setting, why do RPGs <i>also</i> have traditional difficulty settings?  What do they mean in relation to one another?  I think it's best to start by thinking about different personas (hypothetical players) and why they may choose different difficulty settings and different amounts of grinding.  Imagine that we have an RPG with three difficulty settings: "Easy", "Normal", and "Hard".
</p><ul class="standard-text-interrupt">
    <li>Glenn is a single father with a full-time job who doesn't have much time to game.  He's interested in the story of the game, but doesn't want to invest the time into either grinding or deep strategizing.  He'd rather have a fast-paced RPG that he can blow through in 20 hours.  Therefore, he selects the "Easy" difficulty, skips random encounters regularly, and doesn't grind</li>
    <li>Mildred is a hardcore gamer who wants to overcome the ultimate challenge that the game has to offer.  She sees this as a form of skill expression, so she doesn't want to grind her way out of the challenges, preferring to solve them with good play.  Therefore, Mildred selects the "Hard" difficulty and does a Low Level Game, keeping her levels as low as the game allows.</li>
    <li>Shelley hasn't thought about any of these things and just wants to play the game.  She trusts the developers to guide her to a fun experience and selects the "Normal" difficulty.  Occasionally, Shelley runs into roadblocks, which she solves in the most natural way.  Sometimes she'll fix obvious flaws in her strategy, but she'll grind a level or two if she doesn't see what else to do immediately.</li>
    <li>Timmy is eight years old and loves the power fantasy of crushing his opponents with as much power as possible.  He wants to wipe every random encounter in one attack and every boss in two.  Timmy select the "Easy" difficulty and grinds levels at every turn.</li>
    <li>Jocelyn has a lot of time on her hands thanks to a global pandemic and wants to overcome the hardest challenges that the game has to offer.  However, she typically gets frustrated when she loses to a boss and doesn't know what to do, so she has never developed the skills needed to solve the boss fights with pure strategy.  For this reason, Jocelyn selects the "Hard" difficulty, but grinds on a regular basis when she hits a roadblock.</li>
</ul><p class="standard-text">

Looking at this diagram, something interesting immediately becomes apparent: difficulty in an RPG is two-dimensional!  The vertical axis here represents the amount of overall effort required to beat the game, regardless of whether said effort is invested into time investment or skill.  Since the only way to control this in our example is by changing the difficulty setting, there are only three discrete buckets that the players fall into.  The meaning of the horizontal axis is more complicated.  Assuming that we have already decided how much overall effort is required, the horizontal axis describes how much of that effort will come in the form of improving the player's skill and how much will come in the form of a larger time investment.  Since leveling is granular, the position of each persona along this axis is correspondingly granular.

Of course, not every game supports every part of this grid.  One game that does is Bravely Default, which has the same difficulty options as I've listed and gives players the tools to succeed at low levels while also giving them the opportunity to reach insane power at high levels.  Many RPGs (especially older ones) don't have traditional difficulty settings at all, but this doesn't mean that they ignore the grid entirely.  It just means that players don't get any flexibility on where they sit on the vertical axis.  A game like Final Fantasy 6 lies pretty low on the vertical axis, while a game like Dark Souls is infamous for being on the upper end (I can't speak to this myself since I haven't played it).  Both are acceptable; not every game <i>needs</i> a difficulty slider.  Sometimes a game is designed to push players to their limits even if those players wouldn't push <i>themselves</i> to their limits.  Other games may be designed as RPG comfort food and have fundamental flaws that will show up if players are forced to optimize more, but don't need to be corrected for the intended experience to shine.

Restrictions for where somebody sits on the horizontal axis are less common and harder to do well, but they do exist.  A bad example would be Dragon Warrior, which simply doesn't give players the strategic tools to win battles while underleveled.  Almost any challenge in the game must be overcome by grinding, so players must sit towards the right on the horizontal axis.  There's no good reason for this; the game just isn't fleshed out to the extent that a modern RPG would be.  An example of a more purposeful restriction would be in MMORPGs.  While I haven't played any MMORPGs myself and don't have much experience with them, <a href="https://www.youtube.com/@JoshStrifeHayes">Josh Strife Hayes</a> has posited that one reason why MMORPGs are so grind-heavy is because they're as much of a framework for social interaction as they are a game.  When viewed through this lens, it makes sense that an MMORPG would force players further to the right.

On the other side of the coin, we have games that force players to improve to win.  One example would be the RPG Maker game <a href="https://farawaytimes.itch.io/facets">Facets</a>, which has no leveling system.  Completing the game requires you to ration the resources at your disposal correctly to avoid running out.  The is about as far left as you can get.  It's still possible to incorporate a level system into a game that doesn't allow you to grind your way out of problems though.  A good example would be the <a href="https://www.nexusmods.com/finalfantasyxx2hdremaster/mods/218">Final Fantasy 10 Masters Mod</a>, which only awards EXP for completing boss fights.  This maintains a sense of progression and introduces another resource (sphere levels) that you need to spend carefully.  Of course, there are some bad examples too, like the RPG Maker game "100-Level Dungeon" that Graeldon played in a recent <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0rRPJF431b8">video</a>.  Due to poor balance, it's probably only possible to beat this game with specific class choices, which means that you can't customize the party to fit your playstyle and you need some trial and error to win.  You could perhaps pin this issue on the balance itself, but the inability to grind certainly makes it worse.  All of these games are about as far right as you can go on the horizontal axis.

There <i>are</i> some games that simply don't fit into this paradigm.  In Final Fantasy 8, enemies scale to your level.  Because most improvements to your party come from improving your Junctions, increasing your level boosts the enemies more than it does your party.  You instead want your party to be at a low level, where your great Junctions will overshadow your poor natural stats and the enemies' stats will still be low.  This reverses the dynamic between time investment and strategy.  If you spend more time grinding, your "reward" is that you'll usually need to play better to win too.  This is... certainly a decision.  I don't like that the game incentivizes skipping all the random encounters, and this is one (definitely not the only) big reason why I don't like Final Fantasy 8's design much.  I'm not sure if it could truly work well to sit outside this difficulty paradigm or if that approach is fundamentally flawed.  If it could work, it might need to be in an avant-garde project where the odd mechanics are integrated with the story somehow.  The RPG equivalent of Spec Ops: The Line, perhaps?

To circle back to the beginning, each axis of the grid represents a different way to think about RPG difficulty.  The vertical axis is intrinsic to the game itself, while the horizontal axis is intrinsic to how the player decides to play the game.  Both types of difficulty are important to think about, but you only get the full picture when you consider both in tandem.
